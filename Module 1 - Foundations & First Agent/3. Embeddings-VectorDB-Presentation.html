📐Part 1

Embeddings — Turning Meaning Into Numbers

🧭What Is an Embedding?

The Definition

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents the meaning of something — a word, sentence, document, image, or audio clip — in a way machines can compare mathematically.

The Mental Model: A "Meaning Coordinate"

Think of every piece of content as a point on a map. Items with similar meaning sit close together; items with different meanings sit far apart. The coordinates of that point are the embedding.

A Banking Example

Take three customer queries:

  • "How do I reset my password?" → vector A
  • "I forgot my login credentials" → vector B (very close to A)
  • "What's my current loan EMI?" → vector C (far from A & B)

A keyword search would never connect "reset password" and "forgot credentials" — they share no words. But their embeddings sit right next to each other in vector space because they mean nearly the same thing.

The Key Property

Embeddings turn the fuzzy concept of "similar meaning" into something concrete: distance in a high-dimensional space. Once you have that, you can search, cluster, classify, and recommend — all using simple geometry.

❓Why Are Embeddings Needed?

The Problem They Solve

Computers natively work with numbers, not language. Before embeddings, the workarounds were poor:

  • Exact matching — "fraud" doesn't match "fraudulent" or "scam"
  • Keyword counting (TF-IDF) — "loan rejection" and "credit denial" look unrelated despite identical meaning
  • Rules & regex — impossibly brittle for natural language

Where Embeddings Show Up in Banking

Use Case What Embeddings Enable
Customer-support search Find the right policy doc even when query wording differs from doc wording
Compliance Q&A over RBI circulars Match plain-English questions to dense regulatory language
KYC document deduplication Detect that two PAN-card scans of the same person are actually duplicates
Customer-complaint clustering Group thousands of complaints into emerging themes — no labels needed

The bottom line: if you have any task that depends on understanding what text means rather than what it literally says — you need embeddings.

🔬Anatomy of an Embedding Vector

An embedding is just a fixed-length array of floating-point numbers. The length is called the dimension.

Example: Embedding the sentence "Loan was denied"

Common Dimensions in 2026

384, 768, 1024 — small/fast models (BGE-small, MiniLM)
1536 — OpenAI text-embedding-3-small, default for many
3072 — OpenAI text-embedding-3-large, Gemini Embedding 001
4096+ — Specialized high-precision models (rare; expensive storage)

👑Interpreting the Embedding Space

The Famous Demonstration

The most cited result from Mikolov's 2013 Word2Vec paper:
vec("king") − vec("man") + vec("woman") ≈ vec("queen")

Geometric operations on embedding vectors preserve semantic relationships. This was the moment AI researchers realized embedding spaces have real structure.

What Other Analogies Work?

The same pattern works for many semantic relationships learned from co-occurrence patterns:

  • Paris − France + Germany ≈ Berlin (capital-of relation)
  • walking − walk + swim ≈ swimming (verb-tense relation)
  • bigger − big + small ≈ smaller (comparative relation)

Caveat: results aren't always clean — they depend heavily on the corpus, model, and ambiguity of the words.

The Selection Framework (5 Criteria)

  1. Accuracy requirements — MTEB scores; benchmark on YOUR data
  2. Language support — English-only vs multilingual vs Indic
  3. Cost constraints — API per-call cost + vector DB storage cost (function of dim)
  4. Latency needs — milliseconds matter for real-time search
  5. Multimodal needs — text + image + audio in same space?

Don't pick by leaderboard alone. Generic MTEB scores often don't translate to your domain. Always evaluate 2-3 candidate models on your own data with realistic queries before committing — switching later means re-indexing your entire corpus.

📏Dimensionality: More Isn't Always Better

An embedding's dimension is just the length of the vector. The trade-offs of choosing higher vs lower dim are real and often surprising.

✅ Higher Dimensions (e.g., 3072)

Pros:

  • Slightly more semantic nuance
  • Often higher MTEB retrieval scores
  • Better at distinguishing subtle differences

Cons:

  • 2-8× more storage cost (GB compounds at scale)
  • Slower nearest-neighbor search
  • Higher RAM needs for in-memory indexes
  • Marginal quality gains beyond 768-1024 dims

⚡ Lower Dimensions (e.g., 384-768)

Pros:

  • Faster search (lower latency p99)
  • Cheaper storage at scale
  • Less RAM for in-memory indexes
  • Often "good enough" for retrieval

Cons:

  • Slightly lower nuance / accuracy
  • May struggle with fine-grained distinctions

Concrete Storage Numbers

Documents Chunks (1/doc) Dim 384 Dim 768 Dim 1536 Dim 3072
10,000 (~policy library) 10K vectors 15 MB 30 MB 60 MB 120 MB
100K (medium corpus) 100K vectors 150 MB 300 MB 600 MB 1.2 GB
1M (large bank) 1M vectors 1.5 GB 3 GB 6 GB 12 GB
10M (enterprise scale) 10M vectors 15 GB 30 GB 60 GB 120 GB

Assumes float32 (4 bytes per dimension). Quantization to int8 cuts these by 4× with minor quality loss.

The Matryoshka Trick (MRL)

Modern models like OpenAI text-embedding-3 and Gemini Embedding 001 support Matryoshka Representation Learning: you can truncate the vector to a smaller dim (e.g., 1024 from 3072) and it still works — quality degrades gracefully. Lets you trade dim ↔ quality per use case.

🏗️How Are Embedding Models Trained? (Briefly)

You don't need to train embeddings yourself — pre-trained models are excellent. But understanding the principle helps you debug, evaluate, and pick the right model.

The Core Training Idea: Contrastive Learning

Show the model massive batches of text pairs and teach it three things at once:

  1. Pull together: Pairs that should be similar → make their vectors closer
  2. Push apart: Pairs that should be dissimilar → make their vectors farther
  3. Ignore: Unrelated random pairs serve as "negatives" to provide contrast

Training Data Sources

Source Type Example Signal
Question-Answer pairs Stack Overflow, Quora, customer support logs Question and its accepted answer should be close
Paraphrases / translations News headline variants, MT-bitext, paraphrase corpora Same meaning, different wording → should be close
Anchor + retrieved doc MS MARCO, BEIR datasets Query and relevant retrieved passages should be close
Hard negatives Mined: similar-looking but actually wrong matches Look similar but mean different → should be far apart
Adjacent paragraphs Wikipedia sections, book chapters Nearby text in same doc → topically related

Fine-Tuning for Your Domain

Off-the-shelf models work well for general English. But for specialized domains (banking jargon, legal contracts, medical terms), fine-tuning on domain pairs can give 10-30% retrieval gains.

How: collect 1,000-10,000 (query, relevant_doc) pairs from your real usage. Use sentence-transformers library to fine-tune BGE/E5/SBERT for a few hours on a single GPU. Costs <$100; payoff is permanent.

📐Part 2

Similarity Math — How Embeddings Are Compared

📏How Do You Compare Two Vectors?

Once text is embedded into vectors, comparing meaning becomes a math problem. Three distance metrics dominate.

Metric Formula (intuition) What It Measures When To Use
Euclidean Distance (L2) √Σ(aᵢ − bᵢ)² Straight-line distance between two points in space When vector magnitude matters; small vectors only
Cosine Similarity (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖) Angle between vectors, ignores their length Default for text embeddings — meaning, not magnitude
Dot Product Σ(aᵢ × bᵢ) Mix of angle AND magnitude When embeddings are already normalized (then = cosine)

Why Cosine Wins for Text Embeddings

Text embedding models usually normalize vectors to unit length (length = 1). Once normalized, the only meaningful difference between two vectors is the direction they point — which is exactly what cosine similarity measures.

For unit-normalized vectors: cosine similarity = dot product. So most production vector DBs default to one of these two (they're mathematically identical when normalized).

Quick Banking Intuition

Imagine each loan-default risk report as an arrow pointing in some direction in "topic space". A report about "auto loans in Tier-2 cities" points one way; a report about "credit-card defaults in metros" points another. Cosine similarity measures the angle between these arrows — how aligned the underlying themes are.

📐Cosine Similarity: The Formula & Geometric Picture

The Formula

cos(θ) = A · B ‖A‖ × ‖B‖

where A · B = dot product (sum of element-wise products), and ‖A‖ = magnitude (Euclidean length) of A

Interpretation Scale

Cosine Score Angle θ Interpretation
+1.00°Identical meaning
+0.8 to +0.99~10-37°Very similar (paraphrases, synonyms)
+0.5 to +0.8~37-60°Related topics, same domain
+0.2 to +0.5~60-78°Loosely related
0.090°Unrelated (orthogonal)
−1.0180°Theoretically opposite (rarely seen in practice)

Note: in practice, normalized text embeddings almost never go below ~0.1 — natural language always shares some structure.

🔎Semantic Search: End-to-End Workflow

Cosine similarity over embeddings is the engine that powers semantic search. Here's how the pieces fit together at query time:

⚔️Semantic Search vs. Keyword Search

🔤 Keyword Search (BM25 / TF-IDF)

How it works: Match exact words (or stems). Score by term frequency × inverse document frequency.

✅ Strengths:

  • Lightning fast (decades of tuning)
  • Excellent for exact-match queries: IDs, names, error codes, ticker symbols, account numbers
  • Explainable — you can show why a result matched
  • Strong on rare technical terms model embeddings might miss

❌ Weaknesses:

  • "Loan rejected" ≠ "credit denied" (no shared words)
  • Misspellings break it
  • No understanding of synonyms or paraphrases

🧠 Semantic Search (Vector Embeddings)

How it works: Embed query & documents into vectors; rank by cosine similarity.

✅ Strengths:

  • Understands meaning — "credit denied" matches "loan rejected"
  • Handles paraphrases and synonyms naturally
  • Multilingual (cross-language search!)
  • Tolerant of misspellings

❌ Weaknesses:

  • Slower; needs vector DB infrastructure
  • Can MISS exact-match queries (e.g., account #12345)
  • "Similar" embeddings ≠ "relevant" — false positives
  • Black box — hard to explain why two things matched

🏆 The 2026 Winner: Hybrid Search

The production answer is almost never one or the other. It's both. Run BM25 in parallel with vector search, then fuse the rankings using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). This catches:

  • Exact product codes, account IDs, ticker symbols (BM25 strength)
  • Paraphrased and conceptually-related queries (vector strength)

We'll see hybrid search architecture in detail in Part 4. Just remember: "pure semantic" is rarely optimal.

🚧Part 3

The Limits That Force Us to RAG

📝Limits of Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering is powerful — but it can only work with what the LLM already knows and what you can fit into the context. That hard ceiling shows up fast in BFSI use cases.

Where Prompt Engineering Falls Short

Limitation Banking Example
Knowledge cutoff "Latest RBI Master Direction on digital lending" — the model was trained months/years ago; it doesn't know the latest amendments
Private & proprietary data "What's our bank's specific KYC policy for NRI accounts?" — model has never seen your internal docs
Hallucination on facts Asked for "Section 17.3 of the IRDAI insurance code", the model confidently invents text that sounds regulatory but doesn't exist
No source attribution "Where did you get that interest rate from?" — model can't cite; auditor rejects the answer
Fitting large docs You can stuff a 200-page loan agreement into a 1M-token context — but you'll pay $$$ per query, lose attention quality, and still miss the answer (next slides)
Multi-document reasoning "Compare these 30 customer complaints to last quarter's pattern" — even with huge context, the model gets distracted
Freshness in dynamic data Current FX rates, real-time market data, today's transactions — context window is always a snapshot

The pattern: Prompt engineering tells the model how to answer. It can't give the model new knowledge. For that, you either fine-tune (slow, expensive, narrow) or you retrieve relevant context at runtime — which is exactly what RAG does.

📜"Just Use a Long-Context LLM!" — The Promise vs. Reality

By 2026, you can put 1-2 million tokens into a single LLM call. So why not just dump all your bank's policies, manuals, and circulars in the prompt and call it a day?

The Surface-Level Promise

  • Gemini 3.1 Pro: 2,000,000 tokens (~1,500 pages, ~6 novels)
  • Claude Sonnet 4.6: 1,000,000 tokens
  • GPT-4.1: 1,000,000 tokens

"Just put your whole knowledge base in the prompt — no RAG needed, right?" Wrong.

The Three Hidden Realities

  1. Cost explodes linearly. 500K input tokens at $3/M = $1.50 per query. At 100K queries/month, that's $150K/month just for input — most of which is irrelevant to any single answer.
  2. Latency degrades. A 500K-token prompt can take 30-60 seconds before the model starts generating. Unacceptable for any real-time use.
  3. Quality silently degrades. The "Lost in the Middle", "Context Rot", and "Distractor" effects (next 3 slides) mean the model's effective performance is FAR worse than the context window suggests.

The empirical truth: A focused 5,000-token prompt with the right 5 retrieved chunks outperforms a 500,000-token prompt containing the entire corpus — at 1/100th the cost and 1/30th the latency. RAG isn't a workaround for short contexts — it's a better strategy even when you have long ones.

Cost example based on Claude Sonnet 4.6 pricing ($3/M input). Latency from Anthropic and OpenAI published benchmarks, 2026.

🎯The "Lost in the Middle" Problem

What It Is

When LLMs process long context, they pay much more attention to information at the start and end than to information in the middle. Put the right answer in the middle of 100 pages, and the model often misses it entirely — even with massive context windows.

Why Does This Happen?

  • Causal masking (architectural): The decoder transformer's causal attention naturally weighs recent tokens more — the end position is privileged.
  • Training distribution: Most training examples had important info at the start ("Summarize this article…") or end ("…what's the answer?")
  • Attention dilution: The model's attention budget gets spread thinner across more tokens as context grows.

The implication for RAG: Even if you can fit everything in context, retrieving the most relevant chunks and placing them at the start/end of the prompt is structurally better than dumping the entire corpus. RAG isn't just about cost — it's about positional advantage.

Original research: Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (Stanford/UC Berkeley, TACL 2023). MIT 2025 follow-up explained the causal-masking mechanism. No production model has fully eliminated this bias as of 2026.

🦠"Context Rot" — The Silent Killer

The Phenomenon

Context rot is the measurable degradation in LLM output quality as input length grows — even when the context window isn't close to full. More tokens in → worse output out.

The 2025 Research That Made It a Named Problem

In July 2025, Chroma Research published "Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance" by Kelly Hong, Anton Troynikov, and Jeff Huber. They tested 18 frontier models — GPT-4.1, Claude 4, Gemini 2.5, Qwen3, and variants — on extended Needle-in-a-Haystack tasks.

Conclusion: every single model degrades as input grows. Not some. Not most. All 18 of them.

Why It Happens (3 Compounding Mechanisms)

Mechanism What's Going Wrong
1. Lost-in-the-middle Position bias — info in the middle gets ignored (slide 23)
2. Attention dilution Finite attention budget spread across more tokens. Info attended at 1K tokens may be functionally ignored at 100K tokens.
3. Distractor interference Semantically similar but irrelevant content actively misleads the model. A single distractor reduced accuracy; four compounded the damage. (Next slide.)

The Critical Distinction: Context Rot ≠ Context Overflow

Overflow happens when you exceed the model's maximum token limit. Rot happens well before that. A model with a 200K token window can show significant degradation at 50K tokens. The decline is continuous, not a cliff.

This is why "my context window is big enough" is the wrong metric. What matters is signal-to-noise ratio.

Source: Chroma Research, "Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance" (Hong, Troynikov, Huber — July 2025). 18 frontier models tested. The term "context rot" was formalized in this paper.

🎭Distractor Interference: When Lookalikes Mislead

The Effect Few People Discuss

Beyond positional bias, Chroma's 2025 research surfaced another silent failure mode: distractor interference. When the long context contains passages that look similar to the answer but aren't actually relevant, the model gets confused — and confidence drops faster than context length alone would predict.

A BFSI Illustration

Query: "What is the foreign-currency remittance limit for residents under LRS for 2025-26?"

Context dumped: 500 pages of RBI circulars, including:

  • ✅ The actual current LRS limit ($250,000) — page 240
  • ⚠️ Distractor: Historical LRS limit from 2014 ($125,000) — page 47
  • ⚠️ Distractor: Capital-account remittance limits (different rule) — page 110
  • ⚠️ Distractor: NRI repatriation limits (different category) — page 300
  • ⚠️ Distractor: Corporate ODI limits (different rule) — page 412

What the LLM may do: Get distracted by lookalikes and answer with an outdated or wrong limit — confidently.

The Compounding Effect

Chroma found: 1 distractor dropped accuracy below the baseline. 4 distractors compounded the drop dramatically — much worse than context length alone would predict.

This matters hugely for BFSI: regulatory text is full of lookalikes (amended sections, repealed clauses, historical schedules, related-but-different jurisdictions). Long-context isn't enough — you need targeted retrieval of just the relevant sections.

This is exactly why RAG with good retrieval & reranking outperforms long-context dumps: retrieval is a quality filter. Fewer, more-relevant chunks beat more, less-relevant ones — every time.

Source: Chroma Research, 2025 Context Rot study. The distractor finding is one of the most underappreciated results — most teams optimizing RAG don't realize that adding more retrieved context can hurt if relevance isn't strict.

🪡"Needle in a Haystack" Tests — And Their Limits

What the Test Does

The Needle in a Haystack (NIAH) benchmark inserts a single random sentence ("the needle") into a long irrelevant document ("the haystack"), then asks the LLM to retrieve it. Example: hide "The best thing to do in San Francisco is eat a chocolate-covered ice cream on a sunny day" in a 100K-token essay, then ask: "What is the best thing to do in SF?"

What It Reveals — and What It Misses

Aspect What Vanilla NIAH Tests What It Misses
Single-fact retrieval ✅ Can the model find one isolated piece of info? ❌ Real queries often need multiple facts combined
Lexical overlap ✅ Needle & query share keywords ❌ Real queries are paraphrased; needles are buried in similar text
No distractors Haystack is random irrelevant text ❌ Real corpora have lookalikes & conflicting facts (slide 25)
One-hop reasoning The needle IS the answer ❌ Real queries need to combine multiple needles (multi-hop)

Harder Successors

  • Multi-needle benchmarks — multiple needles must be combined
  • Reasoning-needle: "Yuki lives next to Semper Opera House" + the model must infer that's in Dresden to answer "Who's been to Dresden?" — much harder
  • LONGPIBENCH (2025): Tests positional bias with multiple relevant pieces at varied distances
  • Chroma's extended NIAH: Adds distractors, paraphrasing, semantic-overlap traps

Modern Models Pass Vanilla NIAH — That Doesn't Mean They're Reliable on Long Context

By 2024, frontier models (Claude 3+, GPT-4+, Gemini 1.5+) scored near-perfect on vanilla NIAH. This was widely celebrated as "solved". But Chroma's 2025 study showed harder variants still fail badly. NIAH is the simplest possible long-context test — not a reliable proxy for production performance.

📊Advertised vs. Effective Context Window (MECW)

The Concept

The Maximum Effective Context Window (MECW) is a model's real performance ceiling — the input length beyond which quality drops unacceptably. Coined in research by Norman Paulsen (2025), MECW is almost always far smaller than the advertised context window.

The Gap Is Often Massive

Research shows effective context can fall below the advertised maximum by up to 99% on complex tasks. Attention degrades non-linearly past the MECW, and context rot begins well before the advertised limit.

Model Advertised Window Typical Effective Window* Practical Gap
Gemini 3.1 Pro 2,000,000 tokens ~100-200K (complex tasks) ~10-20×
Claude Sonnet 4.6 1,000,000 tokens ~100-200K ~5-10×
GPT-4.1 1,000,000 tokens ~80-150K ~7-12×
GPT-5.5 270,000 tokens ~50-100K ~3-5×

*"Effective" varies wildly by task. Simple lookup may approach the advertised window. Complex multi-step reasoning with distractors may degrade much earlier.

The Practical Rule of Thumb

For production work in 2026:

  • Up to ~10K tokens: Pretty safe; modern models handle this well
  • 10K-100K tokens: Quality starts to vary by model and task; benchmark on your data
  • 100K+ tokens: Context rot is real and material; expect inconsistent quality
  • 500K+ tokens: Marketing more than engineering. Use only for tasks with low precision requirements (broad summarization, vibe-check).

MECW concept: Paulsen, "Maximum Effective Context Window" (2025). Effective window estimates synthesized from Chroma Context Rot research, LONGPIBENCH, and community benchmarks. Numbers are illustrative — exact MECW depends heavily on task type.

🎯So Why RAG? The Case Built So Far

We've now seen five reasons why "just put it in the prompt" doesn't work — and each one is a direct argument for retrieval-based architectures.

Problem How RAG Solves It
1. Knowledge cutoff / private data Retrieve fresh, proprietary content at query time. Update the corpus — no model retraining needed.
2. Hallucination on facts Ground the LLM's answer in retrieved passages with citations. Auditable, verifiable.
3. Cost explosion with long context Send only the 5-10 most relevant chunks (~5K tokens) instead of 500K. 100× cost reduction.
4. Lost-in-the-middle & positional bias Short, focused prompts put critical info in privileged positions (start/end). Quality stays high.
5. Context rot & distractor interference High signal-to-noise ratio. Only relevant passages reach the LLM — distractors are filtered out by retrieval.

The RAG Mental Model

Instead of "put everything in the prompt and hope the model finds the answer",
RAG says: "find the answer first, then put just that in the prompt".

It's a fundamental shift from "context as dumping ground" to "context as curated working memory".

💾Part 4

Vector Databases — The Infrastructure of Retrieval

💾What Is a Vector Database?

The Definition

A vector database is a database specifically optimized for storing high-dimensional vectors and finding the closest matches to a query vector — fast, at scale, with rich filtering and metadata support.

Why Can't We Just Use PostgreSQL or MongoDB?

You technically can store vectors in any database. But traditional databases choke on the core operation: finding the k nearest neighbors among millions of vectors. Why?

  • No indexing for high-dim space: B-tree, hash, GIN indexes don't help in 1500-dim space
  • Curse of dimensionality: Distances become less meaningful as dimensions grow — naive approaches fail
  • O(N) brute-force scaling: Comparing query to every vector → linear cost in DB size
  • Filtering + similarity together: "Find similar docs WHERE department = 'compliance'" needs joint optimization

Vector DBs solve this by combining specialized indexes (HNSW, IVF, LSH) with traditional metadata filtering. The result: millisecond k-NN search across billions of vectors.

What a Vector DB Actually Stores

id vector (1536 dims) metadata (filterable JSON) text ------- ------------------------- ----------------------------- ------------------- chunk_001 [0.024, -0.187, 0.451, ...] {dept: "compliance", date: "Master Direction on "2024-08-15", source: KYC norms states..." "RBI_master_direction.pdf"} chunk_002 [-0.118, 0.301, -0.244, ...] {dept: "lending", date: "Loan-to-value ratio for "2025-01-30", source: housing loans..." "rbi_circular_lending.pdf"}

Three things per row: (1) the embedding vector, (2) filterable metadata, (3) the original text so you can return the actual chunk after similarity search.

🔬"Vector Store" vs. "Vector Database" — A Subtle but Important Distinction

The terms are used interchangeably in casual conversation, but in serious engineering, they're different things. Understanding the gap helps you pick the right tool.

📚 Vector Store (Library)

A library you import into your application. Holds vectors in memory or on local disk, performs similarity search.

What it has:

  • k-NN / ANN algorithms
  • In-process API
  • Save/load to disk

What it lacks:

  • ❌ Network access (it's a library, not a server)
  • ❌ Concurrent multi-user writes
  • ❌ Transactions, ACID, replication
  • ❌ Built-in scaling, sharding
  • ❌ Access control, user auth

Examples: FAISS, Annoy, ScaNN, HNSWlib, nmslib

🗄️ Vector Database (Server)

A full database system — runs as a service, accessed over the network, manages persistence, scaling, concurrency.

What it adds on top of a store:

  • ✅ Network API (REST, gRPC)
  • ✅ Multi-tenant, concurrent writes
  • ✅ Persistence with WAL / replication
  • ✅ Distributed scaling, sharding
  • ✅ Auth, RBAC, encryption at rest
  • ✅ Backup, restore, monitoring
  • ✅ Hybrid search (vectors + metadata)
  • ✅ CRUD operations on vectors

Examples: Pinecone, Qdrant, Weaviate, Milvus, Chroma (in server mode), pgvector

The Practical Rule

  • Prototyping / small static dataset (<100K vectors): A vector store like FAISS is perfect. Zero ops overhead.
  • Production, multi-user, frequently updated data: A real vector database. You need the operational features.
  • FAISS is a store, NOT a database — common confusion. People run FAISS in production but pay for it in operational pain (no concurrent writes, manual persistence, no replication).

🔍Similarity Search Techniques: The Big Picture

The core job of a vector DB is to answer: "Given query vector q, find the k closest vectors in my collection." There are several fundamentally different ways to do this — each with its own trade-offs.

⚪ Exact k-NN (brute force) — slow but perfect; baseline for small datasets
🟦 ANN: HNSW — graph-based; default in most modern vector DBs
🟪 ANN: IVF + variants — clustering-based; good for very large datasets
🟥 ANN: LSH / Product Quantization — memory-efficient; good for billions
🟧 Keyword search (BM25 / TF-IDF) — non-vector; matches exact words
🟢 Hybrid search — combines BM25 + vector with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)
🔷 Graph-based retrieval (GraphRAG) — knowledge-graph-aware retrieval

How Production Systems Actually Combine These

The 2026 production standard for serious RAG is rarely "just vector search". It's almost always a multi-stage pipeline:

  1. Cast a wide net: Hybrid (BM25 + HNSW vector search) retrieves top 50 each
  2. Fuse the rankings: Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) merges into top 100 candidates
  3. Refine with a reranker: Cross-encoder reranks to top 10 by true relevance
  4. Pass to LLM: Top 5-10 chunks become the context

We'll explore each layer in the next few slides.

🎯Exact k-NN (Brute Force) — When Perfect Recall Matters

How It Works

For each query, compute the distance to every single vector in the collection, then sort and return the top k. Mathematically guaranteed to find the true nearest neighbors.

The Trade-Off

Dataset Size Time per query (1536-dim, single CPU) Verdict
1,000 vectors ~1 ms ✅ Instant
10,000 vectors ~10 ms ✅ Fine
100,000 vectors ~100 ms ⚠️ Slow for interactive use
1,000,000 vectors ~1 second ❌ Unacceptable
10,000,000 vectors ~10 seconds ❌ Impossible for real-time

Numbers approximate; depend heavily on hardware, vectorization (AVX-512, GPU), and implementation. GPU brute-force can be much faster at the cost of hardware.

When to Use Exact Search Anyway

  • Small datasets (<100K vectors): Exact is faster than building & querying an index
  • Compliance / audit requirements: "We must guarantee the truly closest match" — exact gives this
  • Ground-truth evaluation: When benchmarking ANN methods, exact is the gold standard for recall@k
  • Re-ranking small candidate sets: After ANN narrows to top-50, exact distance between 50 candidates is cheap and accurate

The reality: For most production RAG (millions of vectors, real-time queries), ANN methods are mandatory. The accuracy loss is minimal (typically >95% recall), and the speed gain is 100-10,000×.

🕸️HNSW: The Dominant ANN Algorithm

Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) is the most widely used ANN algorithm in production vector DBs in 2026. It's the default in Chroma, Qdrant, Weaviate, Milvus, OpenSearch — almost everything.

The Big Idea: A Multi-Layer Graph

Build a graph where each node = a vector, and edges connect vectors that are "close". To search, start at the top layer (sparse, long-range edges) and navigate downward, getting more precise at each layer.

Why HNSW Won the ANN Wars

  • Fast queries: O(log N) — sub-10ms even at millions of vectors
  • High recall: Typically 95-99% recall vs exact search
  • Easy to tune: Two main knobs (M, ef_search) trade speed vs accuracy
  • Incremental: Can add new vectors without rebuilding the whole index

Drawbacks: RAM-hungry (the graph lives in memory). Deletions are expensive. Performance can drift as the graph evolves with many inserts/deletes.

🧰Other ANN Families (Beyond HNSW)

Algorithm How It Works Strengths Drawbacks

(Hierarchical Navigable Small World)		 Multi-layer graph; navigate coarse → fine Fast, high recall, easy to tune; 2026 default RAM-heavy; deletes expensive

The Decision Tree

  • <1M vectors, RAM available: HNSW — easy choice
  • 10-100M vectors, RAM tight: IVF-PQ — memory-efficient
  • Billions of vectors, single node: DiskANN — disk-based
  • Billions across cluster: Milvus or Vespa (typically IVF-PQ + sharding)

🔤Keyword Search: BM25 & the Sparse World

Despite all the vector hype, keyword search isn't dead — and most production RAG systems in 2026 use it alongside vectors. Here's why.

BM25 in One Paragraph

BM25 (Best Matching 25) is a refined version of TF-IDF that's been the workhorse of keyword search for 30 years (Lucene, Elasticsearch, Solr). For each document, it scores how well it matches a query based on:

  • Term Frequency (TF): Words appearing more often in the doc score higher (with saturation — diminishing returns)
  • Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): Words appearing in few docs (rare terms) score higher
  • Length normalization: Long docs aren't unfairly rewarded just for having more text

The result: a sparse representation where each doc is described by which words it contains and how often.

Why Keyword Search Still Matters for BFSI

Banking Query Type Why Vectors Struggle, BM25 Wins
"Section 17.3 of FEMA" Exact section number; vectors blur this with similar-looking sections
"Account 11AABC5566D" Account/PAN/CIN numbers must match exactly — embeddings ignore exact form
"RBI/2024-25/156" Circular reference codes need precise lookup
"NIFTY-12-OCT-25-PE-22000" Option contract strings — vectors hallucinate similarity
"IFSC code SBIN0001234" Unique identifiers — keyword wins, every time

The "Sparse Embedding" Hybrid Approach

Modern systems also use learned sparse representations (SPLADE, BGE-M3's sparse mode) that combine the precision of BM25 with the learning power of neural networks. These can be searched alongside dense vectors in the same index — best of both worlds.

🔀Hybrid Search: BM25 + Vectors + RRF

The 2026 production retrieval pattern is rarely "just BM25" or "just vector". It's hybrid: run both in parallel, then fuse the rankings. The standard fusion algorithm is Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF).

Why RRF Works So Well

  • No score normalization needed — BM25 and cosine produce very different score ranges, but RRF only uses ranks
  • Robust to outliers — one bad score doesn't poison the fusion
  • Simple constant — k=60 is the well-tested default; minimal tuning
  • Boosts items that BOTH methods agree on — those rank #1 by BM25 AND #1 by vector get a big boost

RRF formula from Cormack et al., "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual Rank Learning Methods" (SIGIR 2009). Used by Cohere, OpenAI RAG cookbooks, and nearly every production hybrid search system in 2026.

🕸️GraphRAG: When Relationships Matter More Than Similarity

Sometimes the right answer requires understanding relationships between entities, not just similar text. GraphRAG builds a knowledge graph from your documents and traverses it during retrieval — a fundamentally different approach.

When Vector Search Falls Short — and Graphs Win

Query Type Vector RAG Result GraphRAG Result
Single-fact: "What's the FEMA Section 17.3 limit?" ✅ Fine — retrieves the relevant passage ✅ Same — overkill
Multi-hop: "Which subsidiaries of Adani Group have outstanding loans > ₹100Cr from PSU banks?" ❌ Misses — needs to combine entity relationships ✅ Wins — traverses Company → Subsidiary → Loan → Lender
Network analysis: "Identify shell companies linked via shared directors in this AML investigation" ❌ Misses — relationships are the answer ✅ Wins — graph algorithms for community detection
Thematic: "What are the major themes in customer complaints this quarter?" ⚠️ Partial — gets similar chunks but misses cross-document themes ✅ Wins — GraphRAG community summarization

How Microsoft GraphRAG Works (Briefly)

  1. Extract entities & relationships from documents using an LLM (creates a graph)
  2. Detect communities with the Leiden algorithm — clusters of related entities
  3. Summarize each community using the LLM — pre-computed "themes"
  4. At query time:
    • Local Search for entity-specific questions (uses entity neighborhoods)
    • Global Search for thematic questions (uses community summaries)

The catch: GraphRAG indexing is expensive. For a 100K-chunk corpus, building the graph can consume millions of LLM tokens and take hours to days. Best for static or slow-changing knowledge bases (regulations, technical manuals) — not real-time data.

Source: Microsoft Research GraphRAG (April 2024). Open-source — 31.6K GitHub stars as of 2026. We'll cover GraphRAG in depth in Deck 2 as one of the advanced RAG techniques.

🎯Part 5

Best Practices & Wrap Up

✅Embedding Best Practices

🎯 Do These Things

  • Benchmark 2-3 models on YOUR data — MTEB leaderboards are a starting point, not the answer. Build a small evaluation set of (query, expected-doc) pairs from real usage.
  • Use the SAME embedding model for indexing & querying — different models = different vector spaces = useless similarity. This is the #1 newcomer mistake.
  • Normalize vectors to unit length if your DB doesn't do it automatically. Lets you use dot product (faster) instead of full cosine.
  • Pre-process consistently — same casing, same whitespace, same special-char handling, both at index time and query time.
  • Choose dimension thoughtfully — 768-1024 dim covers most needs. Above 2048, the storage and latency cost rarely justifies the marginal quality gain.
  • Consider Matryoshka models — text-embedding-3 and Gemini Embedding 001 let you truncate vectors and trade quality for speed/storage per use case.
  • Fine-tune on domain pairs if quality matters (10-30% gains for <$100 of GPU time, on BFSI vocabulary)

❌ Avoid These Mistakes

  • Don't compare embeddings from different model families directly
  • Don't pick the highest-dim model "because more is better" — it isn't
  • Don't ignore the cost of re-indexing when changing models — budget for it from day 1
  • Don't trust MTEB v2 scores as comparable to v1 — different benchmarks
  • Don't ignore multilingual implications if you serve Indian-language customers

🗄️Vector Database Best Practices

🎯 Production Practices That Pay Off

  • Default to hybrid (BM25 + vector + RRF) — almost always beats pure semantic. The standard 2026 pattern: top 50 from each → RRF → top 10 to reranker.
  • Add metadata filters early — date, department, document type, compliance tag. Cheap to add, expensive to retrofit.
  • Tune HNSW ef_search per query type — different SLAs need different recall/latency. Critical compliance queries can afford higher ef_search.
  • Use namespaces / collections to partition — separate by tenant (multi-tenant SaaS) or department (loans, compliance, HR). Faster, cleaner, more secure.
  • Replicate for HA in production — at least 2 replicas. Budget for it.
  • Monitor recall@k continuously — keep a small "golden set" of (query, expected-doc) pairs. Run nightly. Drift happens.
  • Plan re-indexing operations — model upgrades, schema changes. Have a runbook before you need it.
  • Consider quantization for storage savings — scalar (int8) or binary quantization shrinks memory 4-32× with minor quality loss

Operational Patterns That Save Pain

  • Store the original text alongside the vector — don't make yourself re-fetch from source storage on every query
  • Tag chunks with source URI + chunk index — for citations and audit trails (regulators love this)
  • Version your chunking strategy — when you change chunk size, you might want to A/B test before fully re-indexing
  • Use deletion via metadata flag, not actual delete — most ANN indexes handle deletions poorly. Soft-delete + periodic compaction is cleaner.

⚠️Common Pitfalls (And How to Avoid Them)

Pitfall Symptom Fix
Different embedding models for indexing vs querying Search returns nothing relevant — looks broken Always use SAME model. Hard-code it in config; alarm if mismatched.
Pure semantic search misses exact IDs "PAN AABCD1234E" doesn't find the exact PAN row Switch to hybrid (BM25 + vector + RRF). Always.
Chunking too small Retrieved chunks lack context to answer the question Chunk size 500-1000 tokens with 10-20% overlap, or use parent-child chunking
Chunking too large Many chunks "kind of" relevant; precision low Smaller chunks (300-500) + reranker on top to bubble best ones
No metadata filters Compliance retrieval returns docs from 2018 mixed with 2025 versions Add date, version, jurisdiction filters at retrieval time
Ignoring multilingual users Hindi/Tamil/Bengali queries return garbage Use multilingual embedding (BGE-M3, Cohere multilingual, mE5)
FAISS in "production" Concurrent writes corrupt index; no replication; data loss Migrate to Qdrant, Weaviate, or pgvector — FAISS is a library, not a DB
Trusting MTEB scores blindly "Best on leaderboard" model performs poorly on your data Benchmark on your domain with real queries before committing
No evaluation set Quality silently drifts; you don't notice until users complain Maintain a small golden set (50-200 queries); run nightly; alert on drops
Over-indexing on cool tech, under-indexing on data quality Fancy GraphRAG / RAG Fusion / etc. — still poor results Garbage in, garbage out. Spend 80% of effort on chunking + cleaning, not on advanced retrieval gimmicks.

🎯Key Takeaways

What You Need to Internalize

About Embeddings

  1. Embeddings turn meaning into geometry. They're the foundational primitive — once text becomes vectors, similarity becomes math.
  2. Choose the model carefully — but don't agonize. 2026 has many strong options. Voyage / Cohere / BGE-M3 / OpenAI text-3 / Gemini Embedding 001 all work. Pick one that fits your constraints (cost, multilingual, multimodal, on-prem), and benchmark on your data.
  3. Dimension is a trade-off, not a quality dial. 768-1024 is the modern sweet spot. Higher only helps in specific cases.

About the Limits That Force RAG

  1. Long context is not a substitute for RAG. Lost-in-the-middle, context rot, and distractor interference all degrade quality long before the context window is full. Even with 1M-token windows, focused retrieval beats brute-force stuffing.
  2. RAG isn't a hack for short contexts — it's a quality strategy. Smaller, more relevant context outperforms larger, less relevant context.

About Vector Databases

  1. HNSW is the 2026 default ANN algorithm. You'll use it whether you know it or not.
  2. Hybrid search (BM25 + Vector + RRF) is the production pattern, not pure semantic.
  3. For most BFSI teams in India, pgvector or Qdrant are the right defaults. Don't reach for Pinecone or Weaviate Cloud unless you've outgrown those.
  4. Vector store ≠ Vector DB. FAISS for notebooks, not for production traffic.
